What makes a leader worth quoting

Thought leadership isn’t about publishing more. It’s about saying something specific that only you could credibly say.

Plenty of executives publish regularly, and very few get quoted. The difference is rarely writing talent. It’s usually whether they’re willing to say something specific.

Have a point of view, not a topic

"The future of our industry" is a topic. A point of view is a position someone could disagree with. If nobody could reasonably argue with what you’ve written, it’s unlikely to change anybody’s mind or interest a journalist.

Speak from experience

The most useful commentary comes from what a leader has seen first-hand: the decision that didn’t work, the pattern they’ve noticed across customers, the assumption their sector keeps getting wrong. That can’t be easily copied, and it’s what makes a voice recognizable.

Be useful to the person writing the story

Journalists need clear answers, relevant evidence, and people who respond while the story’s still live. Being reliably helpful builds relationships that outlast any single article.

Stay consistent

Authority builds through repetition. Choose two or three themes that connect to where the business is going, and return to them. Over time, people start to associate you with those ideas, and that’s when your reputation starts working before you enter the room.
